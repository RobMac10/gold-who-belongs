In 1858 this country was still part of New South Wales. Then the papers reported gold at Canoona, about thirty miles from a new settlement on the Fitzroy River called Rockhampton.

Canoona is on Darumbal Country. Queensland became a separate colony the next year, on 10 December 1859.

Canoona, 1858

A prospector named Chappell (the paper also spelled his name Chappel) told a meeting at Gladstone that the field was payable. Open his report, line by line.

The Moreton Bay Courier · 28 July 1858 · page 2

“One of the first results of the new postal arrangement for a regular periodical communication with Port Curtis is the transmission to us of news of the establishment of a working gold-field there.”

At a meeting in Gladstone on 9 July, Mr Chappell “described the field as about seven miles from the Fitzroy River and 30 miles from Rockhampton.”

He said he had “proved a paying gold field to exist, on which any man not afraid of work could earn his pound a day.”

Source. “MARYBOROUGH. FROM OUR OWN CORRESPONDENT.” The Moreton Bay Courier (Brisbane), 28 July 1858, page 2, reporting a meeting at Gladstone on 9 July. Trove, nla.news-article3725156. The paper printed both “Chappel” and “Chappell”.

See the source (Trove) ↗

Word ran south. By mid-September a Courier writer in Sydney said:

The Moreton Bay Courier · 15 September 1858 · page 2

“All the talk now is of Port Curtis and its gold, and so far as the discovery is concerned, that can be no longer questionable.”

Ships were laid on for the Fitz Roy River: two steamers and nine sailing vessels. “Quite a fleet!”

Source. “NEWS AND NOTES, A SYDNEY MAN.” The Moreton Bay Courier, 15 September 1858, page 2. Trove, nla.news-article3725702.

See the source (Trove) ↗

Thousands came. The field could not hold them. On 16 October the same paper wrote that the mania had nearly passed away, and that money was being raised for “the destitute at Fitz Roy.”

The Moreton Bay Courier · 16 October 1858 · page 2

“The mania, as to the Fitz Roy, has nearly passed away. The gloom of despondency is left to mantle round those who were too sanguine in their expectations.”

Source. Editorial, The Moreton Bay Courier, 16 October 1858, page 2. Trove, nla.news-article3718500. Obvious typographical errors in the Trove text have been corrected (for example “pnssed” to “passed”). The paper’s spelling “Fitz Roy” is left as printed.

See the source (Trove) ↗

Canoona is about forty kilometres north-west of Rockhampton. A University of Queensland summary estimates 15,000 people there between September and December 1858, and says the field was not a “duffer” but could not support that many. The historian Lorna McDonald, quoted by the State Library of Queensland, counted more than 8,000 passengers landing between 1 October and mid-November 1858. This page uses “thousands,” because the exact number is an estimate.

Gympie, 1867

Nine years after Canoona, the new colony of Queensland was in a recession. The government offered a reward for a payable gold field. In October 1867 a prospector, James Nash, wrote from Maryborough to claim it. Open his letter, line by line.

James Nash · letter · Maryborough · 16 October 1867

“Sir,”

“I do myself the honour to inform you that I have found a gold field in the Wide Bay District.”

“It is situated between Curry & Traviston about 50 miles from Maryborough.”

“I have proven several gullys and feel sure there is profitable employment for a large number of miners and I hereby claim the reward offered for the discovery of gold fields by the government.”

“I have the honor to be your obedient servant.”

James Nash

Source. Letter from James Nash to the Minister of Lands, 16 October 1867. Queensland State Archives, item ITM1137542. The wording follows the published transcript of that letter in Gympie Regional Memories (Gympie Regional Libraries). “Curry” is Curra station; “Traviston” is Traveston. The letter keeps Nash’s spellings, including “gullys” and both “honour” and “honor.”

The field was first called Nashville, after Nash. It was later named Gympie. The gold helped the colony through hard times. People later called Gympie the town that saved Queensland.

Gympie is on Gubbi Gubbi / Kabi Kabi Country.

Sources. Queensland State Archives, “Gold at Gympie,” 30 August 2021 (the 1866 recession, the reward, the later town name, and the later wording that hailed the town as the colony’s saviour). Kerr, “Mineral discoveries – as recorded in records at Queensland State Archives,” Journal of Australasian Mining History, vol. 23 (October 2025), says the Executive Council awarded Nash £1,000 in 1868, under a notice of rewards dated 29 January 1867. That amount is not used in the student text, because the letter itself does not name a figure.

Eureka, 1854

Every digger had to buy a licence, whether he found gold or not. Police checked the licences often. Most diggers could not vote.

At Ballarat, late in 1854, the diggers held big meetings on Bakery Hill. On 29 November some of them burned their licences. On Thursday 30 November, after another licence hunt, they gathered under a flag of their own, blue with a white cross and five white stars. Peter Lalor knelt and led them in an oath. Raffaello Carboni, an Italian digger, wrote down the words.

The oath at Bakery Hill · 30 November 1854

“WE SWEAR BY THE SOUTHERN CROSS TO STAND TRULY BY EACH OTHER, AND FIGHT TO DEFEND OUR RIGHTS AND LIBERTIES.”

Source. Raffaello Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (Melbourne, 1855), chapter XXXVII. Carboni printed the words in capital letters.

The diggers built a stockade, a rough fence of wooden slabs, at the Eureka lead. Before dawn on Sunday 3 December, soldiers and police attacked it. The fight was over in a short time. At least twenty-two diggers and six soldiers died.

A preview: who fired first?

Three men wrote about the first shots that morning. Their accounts do not agree. Ask each one the same three questions.

Peter Lalor · letter “To the Colonists of Victoria” · April 1855

“I believe that one or two signal shots were fired by our sentries.”
“The military fired the first volley.”

Source. Peter Lalor, letter “To the Colonists of Victoria”, printed in the Geelong Advertiser (9 April 1855) and The Argus (10 April 1855).

After Eureka

Thirteen men were put on trial in Melbourne for high treason early in 1855. Carboni was one of them. At the end of his trial the jury gave its verdict.

The jury’s foreman · Melbourne · 1855

“NOT GUILTY!”

Source. Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (1855), chapter XC. All thirteen men were found not guilty.

See the source (Project Gutenberg) ↗The trial records (PROV) ↗

Later in 1855 the licence was replaced by a miner’s right. A man who held one could vote. Peter Lalor was elected to the Victorian Parliament. Years later he became its Speaker.

Where the game’s numbers come from

Real: the licence fees and the months they changed; the fine for digging without a licence (£5 to £10, from Clacy); the Forest Creek prices of food and tools; the 2s 6d to steel a pick; gold at 64s an ounce; the export duty of 2s 6d an ounce from 1855; the twelve-foot claim; shafts from ten to eighty feet deep; tradesmen’s pay of one to two pounds a day.

Made up for the game: how much gold you find, how often police, rain, rushes and thieves come, how much food one digger needs each month, and the money you start with. Food each month: Flour, 25 lb at 1s 6d a pound (£1 17s 6d), Meat, 25 lb at 5d a pound (10s 5d), Tea, 1 lb (3s), Sugar, 4 lb at 1s 6d (6s), Candles, 2 lb at 1s 6d (3s). Total £2 19s 11d.

What changed between 1854 and 1857?

In 1854 the census counted 2,341 people in Victoria who were born in China. In 1857 it counted 25,424. Of those, 25,421 were men.

In 1855 the Victorian Parliament passed a law about Chinese arrivals. A ship could carry only one Chinese passenger for every ten tons of its size. And the ship’s master had to pay for every Chinese passenger on board.

An Act to make provision for certain Immigrants · Victoria · 1855

“… the master shall pay to the Collector or other proper Officer of Customs a rate of ten pounds for every such Immigrant arrived in such ship …”

Source. Act No. 39 of 1855 (Victoria), section IV. Transcript: Documenting Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy. In this Act, “Immigrant” meant a man from China.

See the source (Documenting Democracy) ↗

South Australia had no law like this. So ships began to land Chinese passengers at Guichen Bay, beside the small town of Robe. In 1857 thousands of men came ashore there and walked to the goldfields. Tap The walk from Robe to follow it.

REGULATIONS TO BE OBSERVED BY THE PERSONS DIGGING FOR GOLD OR OTHERWISE EMPLOYED AT THE GOLD FIELDS.

  1. This License is to be carried on the person, to be produced whenever demanded by any Commissioner, Peace Officer, or other duly authorised person.
  2. It is especially to be observed that this License is not transferable, and that the holder of a transferred License is liable to the penalty for a misdemeanour.
  3. No Mining will be permitted where it would be destructive of any line of road which it is necessary to maintain, and which shall be determined by any Commissioner, nor within such distance round any store as it may be necessary to reserve for access to it.
  4. It is enjoined that all persons on the Gold Fields maintain a due and proper observance of Sundays.
  5. The extent of claim allowed to each Licensed Miner is twelve feet square, or 144 square feet.

The licence fee, 1851 to 1855

FromWhat a digger paid
September 185130 shillings a month for a licence.
December 1851A rise to £3 a month was announced, to start in January 1852. It was cancelled. On 15 December thousands of diggers met in protest at Forest Creek.
December 1853£1 a month, or £8 for a year. Carboni paid £2 for three months in October 1854.
1855The licence was replaced by the miner’s right, for a year at a time. A duty of 2s 6d an ounce was charged on gold sent out of the colony.

Current prices at Forest Creek
