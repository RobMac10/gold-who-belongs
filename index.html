File GOLD A gold field · a census map · a licence · four investigations · twelve witnesses

Open the file. Dig for gold. Watch who came. Then ask three witnesses the same three questions, four times.

This land's story goes back more than 65,000 years. See Book One.

In 1851 gold was found in New South Wales, and then in Victoria. Within three years Victoria’s population had more than trebled.

They came from Britain and Ireland, from Germany and France, from America and from China, and from the other colonies. They came to dig on Country that already belonged to other people. Ballarat is on Wadawurrung Country. Bendigo, Castlemaine and Forest Creek are on Dja Dja Wurrung Country.

The gold made some people rich. It also raised a question that runs through this file. Who belonged here, and who decided? The diggers asked it about the licence and the vote. Chinese miners met it at Lambing Flat. Kulin people asked it at Coranderrk. Torres Strait Islanders were not asked at all. In the Kimberley, Jandamarra answered it in his own way.

How this works

Play a year on the Ballarat diggings, with the real licence fees and the real prices. Watch the people arrive, using the census counts of 1854 and 1857. Print your own digger’s licence. Then investigate four cases. In each one, read what is agreed, ask three witnesses the same three questions, and write your finding.

What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? “We can’t know” is always an allowed finding, if you can say why.

Eureka, 1854

Every digger had to buy a licence, whether he found gold or not. Police checked the licences often. Most diggers could not vote.

At Ballarat, late in 1854, the diggers held big meetings on Bakery Hill. On 29 November some of them burned their licences. On Thursday 30 November, after another licence hunt, they gathered under a flag of their own, blue with a white cross and five white stars. Peter Lalor knelt and led them in an oath. Raffaello Carboni, an Italian digger, wrote down the words.

Where the game’s numbers come from

Real: the licence fees and the months they changed; the fine for digging without a licence (£5 to £10, from Clacy); the Forest Creek prices of food and tools; the 2s 6d to steel a pick; gold at 64s an ounce; the export duty of 2s 6d an ounce from 1855; the twelve-foot claim; shafts from ten to eighty feet deep; tradesmen’s pay of one to two pounds a day.

What changed between 1854 and 1857?

In 1854 the census counted 2,341 people in Victoria who were born in China. In 1857 it counted 25,424. Of those, 25,421 were men.

In 1855 the Victorian Parliament passed a law about Chinese arrivals. A ship could carry only one Chinese passenger for every ten tons of its size. And the ship’s master had to pay for every Chinese passenger on board.

An Act to make provision for certain Immigrants · Victoria · 1855

“… the master shall pay to the Collector or other proper Officer of Customs a rate of ten pounds for every such Immigrant arrived in such ship …”

Source. Act No. 39 of 1855 (Victoria), section IV. Transcript: Documenting Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy. In this Act, “Immigrant” meant a man from China.

See the source (Documenting Democracy) ↗

South Australia had no law like this. So ships began to land Chinese passengers at Guichen Bay, beside the small town of Robe. In 1857 thousands of men came ashore there and walked to the goldfields. Tap The walk from Robe to follow it.

Investigation 1

Lambing Flat, 1861

What happened on 30 June 1861, and how many people took part?

What is agreed

  • Lambing Flat was a goldfield in New South Wales, on the Burrangong diggings, where the town of Young is now.
  • In 1860 and 1861 crowds of white miners attacked the Chinese miners’ camps there several times and drove them off the field.
  • The worst attack was on Sunday 30 June 1861. The crowd marched with a band, behind a banner painted on a tent flap: “Roll up, Roll up, No Chinese”.
  • Police and soldiers were sent. Some of the miners were put on trial. Most were let go. Two were sent to gaol.
  • In 1862 the government’s commissioner, William Campbell, reported on the Chinese miners’ claims for what they had lost. About £4,240 was paid on 706 of the 1,568 claims.
  • Later in 1861 the New South Wales Parliament passed a law charging ten pounds for each Chinese man who arrived.

An eyewitness letter

A letter from Lambing Flat, printed in The Sydney Morning Herald, 13 July 1861

Source. The Sydney Morning Herald, 13 July 1861, page 5 (Trove).

See the source (Trove) ↗

What did they say they saw? “a body of about two thousand men arrived on Lambing Flat from the new rush, Tipperary Gully, led by a brass band. They carried a large banner, with words “ Roll-up — no Chinese ; ””
The next day: “On Monday morning, there were twelve hundred Chinese out at a Mr. Roberts’ station, three miles from the Flat ; they had neither tents, bedding, nor even a mouthful to eat”

Could they really have seen it? The writer says they were on the Flat that Sunday. “About two thousand” is an estimate, made by someone standing in or near the crowd. The writer also saw the Chinese miners the next morning, at a station three miles away.

Why might they say it? The letter was written for readers in Sydney, a long way from the goldfield. We don’t know who the writer was, or why they chose to write.

The Special Correspondent

A reporter sent by The Sydney Morning Herald · report printed 20 July 1861

Source. The Sydney Morning Herald, 20 July 1861, page 8 (Trove).

See the source (Trove) ↗

What did they say they saw? “upwards of a thousand men, armed with bludgeons and pickhandles, no fire-arms as yet appearing, were assembled round the “No Chinese” standard.”
Later that day: “the number assembled amounted to at least 3000 persons.”
“the Chinese being driven off … without being permitted to take with them any portion of their property.”

Could they really have seen it? His report was printed three weeks after 30 June. He does not say whether he saw the march himself or pieced it together from people who did. His numbers grow during the day, from “upwards of a thousand” to “at least 3000”.

Why might they say it? A newspaper reporter is paid to tell the whole story to readers who were not there. He also describes the attack as carried out “under circumstances of great barbarity in some cases”.

Tom Me, What Young and Que You

Chinese miners at Lambing Flat · petition to the Legislative Assembly of New South Wales, 1861

Source. “Alleged Aggression on Chinese at Lambing Flat: Petition of Tom Me, What Young and Que You”, printed 12 September 1861, Votes and Proceedings of the Legislative Assembly of New South Wales, 1861–62, vol. II, p. 3; as quoted by the Young Historical Museum.

What did they say they saw? “… assembled together on foot and on horseback, to the number, at various times during the said day, of from eight hundred to three thousand, as your Petitioners believe, and being armed with bludgeons, fire-arms, and other weapons, and carrying banners or flags inscribed with the words “Roll up”, “No Chinese,” attacked your Petitioners and the rest of the Chinese, at and near Lambing Flat aforesaid, … drove your Petitioners and the rest of the Chinese, from the said diggings.”

Could they really have seen it? They were among the Chinese miners who were attacked. They saw the crowd from the other side. They give the crowd’s size as a range, “as your Petitioners believe”.

Why might they say it? They were asking the Parliament to pay them for what they had lost. A petition uses the formal words that Parliament expects. We don’t know who wrote the words down for them.

Why it still matters

The banner is kept at the museum in Young. In 2022 it was listed on the New South Wales State Heritage Register. The law passed in 1861 was one of several colonial laws aimed at Chinese arrivals. Forty years later, the new Commonwealth Parliament passed a law to keep out people it did not want.

Teacher note: investigation 1

Conflicts to flag.

  • Crowd size: about 2,000 (letter, SMH 13 July 1861); upwards of 1,000, then at least 3,000 (Special Correspondent, SMH 20 July 1861); 800 to 3,000 (petition). The Young Historical Museum gives 2,000 to 3,000.
  • The petition excerpt is trimmed with ellipses. The full sentence says the petitioners were beaten and that Tom Me’s hair was cut off. The correspondent’s report also describes injuries and plunder. These details are left out of the student text.
  • On 14 July 1861 a miner, William Lupton, was killed when police fired on a crowd at the Lambing Flat police camp. Not in the student text.
  • The trial outcome (“two were sent to gaol”: Owen and Spicer, two years) and the compensation figures (£4,240; 706 of 1,568 claims) follow Campbell’s 1862 report as summarised in published histories; the report itself was not opened from the build machine.
  • The 1861 Act (Chinese Immigrants Regulation and Restriction Act, No. 28a) could not be opened on AustLII from the build machine; it is cited by title.

Images. The banner is a photograph of the object. The letter, correspondent and petition cards are silhouettes, not likenesses.

Investigation 2

Coranderrk, 1863 to 1886

What did the people of Coranderrk want, and what did they say was going wrong?

What is agreed

  • Early in 1863 about forty Kulin people walked over the Dandenong Ranges to an old camping place where Badger Creek meets the Yarra River. Their leaders were Simon Wonga and William Barak.
  • In June 1863 the land was set aside as a reserve, called Coranderrk.
  • The people built houses, cleared paddocks and grew crops. In its early years the station paid its way.
  • After 1874 new managers were stricter. Some members of the Board for the Protection of Aborigines wanted to move the people and sell the land.
  • The people walked to Melbourne again and again with petitions. In 1881 a board of inquiry heard their evidence.
  • In 1886 a new Victorian law made many people of mixed descent aged between fifteen and thirty-five leave stations like Coranderrk. By 1893 thirty-one people lived there. Coranderrk was closed in 1924.

Ask three witnesses

Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? Tap a picture to enlarge it.

William Barak

Wurundjeri leader · lived at Coranderrk from 1863

Source. Evidence to the Coranderrk inquiry, 1881; as quoted by the National Museum of Australia, “Coranderrk”, Defining Moments.

See the source (National Museum of Australia) ↗

What did they say they saw? “And we don’t want any Board nor inspecting Capt. Page over us – only one man, that is Mr. Green, and the station to be under the Chief Secretary; and then we will show to the country that we can work it and make it pay, and I know it will.”
(John Green was the first manager. Captain Page ran the Board. The Chief Secretary was a minister in the Victorian government.)

Could they really have seen it? He had lived at Coranderrk since the walk of 1863 and was one of its leaders. He knew both the early years under Green and the years after. His words were taken down by the inquiry and printed in its report.

Why might they say it? He was speaking to an inquiry that could change what happened to the station. He wanted the people to stay on the land, and to choose who ran it.

Thomas Bamfield

Coranderrk resident · letter handed to the inquiry, 1881

Source. Letter to the Coranderrk inquiry, 1881, approved by Barak; quoted in Diane E. Barwick, Rebellion at Coranderrk (ANU Press), chapter 11.

See the source (ANU Press) ↗

What did they say they saw? “I report this matter for the welfare of the station. The station has never been improved since the old manager left. No clearing or grubbing done; no potatoes, cabbages, or other vegetables have been grown, and no fencing done since he left.”
“Under Mr Green we used to kill our own cattle, and grow our own potatoes, cabbages, onions, carrots and pumpkins – everything we could grow.”

Could they really have seen it? He lived on the station and compared it with the years before Green left, seven years earlier. Barwick writes that Barak approved the letter.

Why might they say it? The letter sums up the residents’ complaints for an inquiry that could bring Green back. Barwick writes that the manager blamed Bamfield for stirring up the complaints.

Anne F. Bon

A supporter of the Coranderrk people · letter to the Chief Secretary, 23 March 1881

Source. Anne F. Bon to Chief Secretary Graham Berry, 23 March 1881; quoted in Barwick, Rebellion at Coranderrk, chapter 11, “Mrs Bon Intervenes”.

See the source (ANU Press) ↗

What did they say they saw? “The blacks are neither slaves nor criminals, then why are they treated as such? You have power to re-appoint the manager they love …”

Could they really have seen it? She was not a resident. She visited the station and knew its people. Later in 1881 she sat on the board of inquiry herself.

Why might they say it? She was writing to a government minister to change who managed the station. A newspaper of the time said the inquiry happened “chiefly” because she kept asking for it.

Why it still matters

The people of Coranderrk used petitions, deputations, letters to newspapers and evidence to an inquiry. For a time in the 1880s they kept their land. The Wurundjeri and other Kulin peoples still tell this story, and Barak is remembered as a leader and an artist.

Investigation 3

Torres Strait, 1879

How did the islands of Torres Strait become part of Queensland, and did the Islanders know?

What is agreed

  • The islands of Torres Strait lie between Cape York and New Guinea. Their peoples had lived there for thousands of years, fishing, gardening, sailing and trading.
  • By 1879 Queensland already held the islands close to its coast. That year the Queensland Parliament passed an Act to take in the rest, all the way to the coast of New Guinea.
  • On 21 July 1879, in Brisbane, the Governor of Queensland made a proclamation. From 1 August 1879 the islands were part of Queensland.
  • Mer, also called Murray Island, is one of the eastern islands. Its people are the Meriam people.
  • In 1992 the High Court of Australia decided the Mabo case. It declared that the Meriam people were entitled to their land on the Murray Islands.

The Governor’s proclamation

Signed at Brisbane, 21 July 1879, under the Queensland Coast Islands Act 1879

Source. Proclamation of 21 July 1879, as quoted in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), Brennan J; Queensland Coast Islands Act 1879, section 1 (Documenting Democracy).

See the source: the Act (Documenting Democracy) ↗The proclamation, in the Mabo judgment (High Court) ↗

What did they say they saw? “that from and after the first day of August, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and seventy-nine, the Islands described in the Schedule … shall be annexed to and become part of the Colony of Queensland, and shall be and become subject to the laws in force therein.”
The Act said the same: “… the Islands described in the schedule hereto shall be annexed to and become part of the Colony of Queensland and shall be and become subject to the laws in force therein.”

Could they really have seen it? It was written in Brisbane, a very long way from the islands. It does not describe the islands or their people at all. It names a date, and a line on a map.

Why might they say it? Before the High Court decided the case, Justice Moynihan of the Supreme Court of Queensland heard the evidence. He found the main purposes of the annexation. They included control of the sea lane through the Strait and of the pearl-shell fishery, protecting ships and shipwrecked crews, bringing everyone on the islands under one law, and protecting the islanders.

Captain Pennefather

Queensland government officer · visited Mer in September 1879

Source. His report, as quoted in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), Brennan J.

See the source (High Court) ↗

What did they say they saw? He reports that he “mustered the natives” and informed them “that they would be held amenable to British law now the island was annexed”.
He also noted: “The Chief acts as magistrate, he has a staff of 10 or 12 men as policemen, they have built a church and courthouse of which they are very proud”

Could they really have seen it? He was on Mer, a month after the annexation took effect. He could see the people, their church and their courthouse. He could not know what they understood by what he told them.

Why might they say it? He was reporting to his superior on the orders he had carried out. His report shows that the Meriam people already had their own leaders and their own system of keeping order.

The High Court of Australia

Mabo v Queensland (No 2), 3 June 1992 · reasons of Justice Brennan

Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2) [1992] HCA 23; (1992) 175 CLR 1, Brennan J.

See the source (High Court) ↗

What did they say they saw? “The people who were in occupation of these Islands before first European contact and who have continued to occupy those Islands to the present day are known as the Meriam people.”
“It may be assumed that on 1 August 1879 the Meriam people knew nothing of the events in Westminster and in Brisbane that effected the annexation of the Murray Islands and their incorporation into Queensland” (Westminster is the British Parliament.)
And: “The acquisition of beneficial ownership of land by the Crown does not appear to have been among the purposes of the annexation” (“Beneficial ownership” means owning the land for its own use.)

Could they really have seen it? The judges were not there in 1879. They wrote 113 years later, from the old records and from the evidence heard in the case, including evidence given by Meriam people.

Why might they say it? The court had to decide whether the Meriam people still held their land, after more than a century of Queensland law. Six of the seven judges agreed that they did.

Why it still matters

The border drawn in 1879 still runs close to the coast of New Guinea. The Mabo case began with Eddie Koiki Mabo and other Meriam people in 1982. After the decision in 1992, the Commonwealth Parliament passed the Native Title Act 1993.

Investigation 4

Jandamarra, 1894 to 1897

Who was Jandamarra, and why do the police and the Bunuba people remember him so differently?

What is agreed

  • The Napier and Oscar Ranges, in the Kimberley in the far north of Western Australia, are Bunuba Country.
  • In 1884 the first sheep station on Bunuba Country was set up at a place the settlers called Lillimooloora. Ten years later it was a police camp.
  • Jandamarra, a young Bunuba man, worked there as a police tracker. The settlers called him Pigeon.
  • In 1894 the constable he worked with was holding Bunuba men as prisoners. Many were Jandamarra’s family. On the night of 31 October Jandamarra set them free. The constable was killed.
  • For almost three years Jandamarra and other Bunuba people fought the police and the settlers from the ranges. Many Bunuba people were killed.
  • On 1 April 1897 Jandamarra was shot dead at Tunnel Creek.

Ask three witnesses

Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? Tap a picture to enlarge it.

George Phillips

Police Commissioner of Western Australia · telegram, 29 February 1896

Source. Telegram of 29 February 1896, as quoted by the National Museum of Australia, Encounters, “West Kimberley, Western Australia”.

See the source (National Museum of Australia) ↗

What did they say they saw? “Pigeon [Jandamarra] and party must be got rid of forthwith. The fact of his being at large is a disgrace to the police. Head the party and let us hear no more of Pigeon.”

Could they really have seen it? He was the head of the police, in Perth, thousands of kilometres from the Kimberley. He never saw Jandamarra. He knew him only from police reports.

Why might they say it? He was giving an order. He calls it “a disgrace to the police” that Jandamarra was still free.

Sub-Inspector Ord

Craven Harry Ord, in charge of the police at Derby · police report, 1890s

Source. Police report, 1890s, as quoted by the National Museum of Australia, Encounters, “West Kimberley, Western Australia”.

See the source (National Museum of Australia) ↗

What did they say they saw? “It would not matter if the whole British army were sent here, [Jandamarra] would still laugh at them from the top of the range.”

Could they really have seen it? He was sent to hunt Jandamarra, so he knew the ranges and how hard the chase was. He did not catch him.

Why might they say it? A police report goes to the officer’s superiors. They wanted results.

June Oscar

Bunuba elder · speaking in 2013 and 2015

Source. As quoted by the National Museum of Australia, Encounters, “West Kimberley, Western Australia”.

See the source (National Museum of Australia) ↗Bunuba Country: Lillimooloora ↗

What did they say they saw? “As far as we’re concerned, Jandamarra lives on. His spirit lives on, his people still live on. His spirit is carried in this country by people who speak the same language as he did.”
In 2015: “This history teaches us that no matter how much we lost ... we were not defeated.”
The Bunuba people’s own website says: “Whilst we paid for this dearly and inevitably bore witness to the invasion of land, the story of Jandamarra speaks to our strength and resilience as a people.”

Could they really have seen it? She was not born in the 1890s. She speaks more than a hundred years later, for the Bunuba people, who have kept this history on their own Country.

Why might they say it? This is how the Bunuba people tell their own story: who they are, and what Jandamarra means to them today.

      Words on the record

      Laws, newspapers and diggers’ books all chose their words. Some words decided who belonged and who did not.

      Each card shows a word as it was written, and the real words around it. Tap “Tap to decode” to find out what it meant and what it did.

      • LicenseA gold licence · Victoria · 1854
        “… I hereby License him to mine or dig for Gold …”

        Source. Gold licence, Victoria, 1854, as reproduced in Raffaello Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (Melbourne, 1855).

        What it meant

        Written permission, paid for. Every digger had to buy one, every month.

        What it did

        Police checked licences on the diggings. Anger at the cost and the searches helped lead to Eureka in 1854.

      • diggersMrs Charles Clacy · 1853
        “… usually in the long run make a fortune quicker than the diggers themselves …”

        Source. Mrs Charles Clacy, A Lady’s Visit to the Gold Diggings of Australia in 1852–53 (London, 1853).

        What it meant

        People who dug for gold.

        What it did

        It became a name for a whole group of people, with its own pride. Later it became a nickname for Australian soldiers.

      • insurgentsCaptain J. W. Thomas · 3 December 1854
        “… were received by rather sharp and well-directed fire from the insurgents …”

        Source. Captain J. W. Thomas, 40th Regiment, report written on the day of the attack. Wikisource transcription.

        What it meant

        Rebels who rise up against the government.

        What it did

        Calling the diggers insurgents made the attack sound like stopping a rebellion. In 1855 juries found the Eureka men “not guilty” of high treason.

      • aliensRaffaello Carboni · 1855
        “… I was the delegate of upwards of one thousand foreigners, or ‘aliens,’ according to the superlative wisdom of your Legislative Council.”

        Source. Raffaello Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (Melbourne, 1855), chapter XXV.

        What it meant

        People from another country who were not British subjects. Carboni was from Italy.

        What it did

        The government’s word marked who was an outsider. Carboni puts it in quotation marks and mocks it.

      • CelestialsRaffaello Carboni · 1855Offensive word · read the teacher note first
        “Had no particular objection to Celestials there …”

        Source. Raffaello Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (Melbourne, 1855), chapter III.

        What it meant

        A name some Europeans used for Chinese people, from “the Celestial Empire”, an old name for China.

        What it did

        It lumped people together by where they came from. It could sound polite, but it marked them as strangers.

      • ImmigrantAn Act of Victoria · 1855
        “And the word “Immigrant” shall mean any male adult native of China or its dependencies or of any islands in the Chinese Seas or any person born of Chinese parents.”

        Source. Act No. 39 of 1855 (Victoria), section I. Transcript: Documenting a Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy.

        What it meant

        An ordinary word, but in this law it meant only men from China.

        What it did

        Ships had to pay ten pounds for every “Immigrant” they brought. The law’s title says only “certain Immigrants”, but it was aimed at one group.

      • Roll-upSydney Morning Herald · 20 July 1861
        “… the cry of “Roll-up” was raised for the first time since the departure of the military.”

        Source. “Riot at Lambing Flat”, Sydney Morning Herald, 20 July 1861, p. 8. Trove.

        What it meant

        A call for miners to gather, fast.

        What it did

        At Lambing Flat the cry gathered crowds who attacked Chinese miners and drove them off the field.

      • roving AboriginesCensus of Victoria · 1857
        “TOTAL POPULATION (exclusive of roving Aborigines)”

        Source. Census of Victoria, 1857, Part VI, Table I. Historical Census and Colonial Data Archive.

        What it meant

        “Roving” means moving from place to place. “Exclusive of” means not counting.

        What it did

        It left Aboriginal people out of the total. The count of who lived in the colony was not a count of everyone.

      • Protection and ManagementAn Act of Victoria · 1869
        “To provide for the Protection and Management of the Aboriginal Natives of Victoria.”

        Source. Aboriginal Protection Act 1869 (Victoria), long title. Transcript: Documenting a Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy.

        What it meant

        “Protection” sounds like keeping people safe. “Management” means control. “Aboriginal Natives” is the law’s name for Aboriginal people.

        What it did

        The Act gave a Board power to decide where Aboriginal people lived and worked, and to make rules about their children.

      • half-casteAn Act of Victoria · 1869 · section 8Offensive word · read the teacher note first
        “Every aboriginal native of Australia and every aboriginal half-caste or child of a half-caste, such half-caste or child habitually associating and living with aboriginals, shall be deemed to be an aboriginal within the meaning of this Act …”

        Source. Aboriginal Protection Act 1869 (Victoria), section 8. Transcript: Documenting a Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy.

        What it meant

        An offensive word the government used for people with Aboriginal and European parents. We use it here only because it is in the law.

        What it did

        It let the government sort people by their parents and decide who came under the Board’s control. The same section let a magistrate decide “on his own view” who was Aboriginal. In 1886 a new law used the same sorting to make many people leave stations like Coranderrk.

      • BoardWilliam Barak · 1881
        “And we don’t want any Board nor inspecting Capt. Page over us – only one man, that is Mr. Green …”

        Source. Evidence to the Coranderrk inquiry, 1881; as quoted by the National Museum of Australia, “Coranderrk”, Defining Moments.

        What it meant

        The Board for the Protection of Aborigines, set up by the 1869 Act.

        What it did

        Barak turns the law’s word around. He says the people do not want the Board. They want to run Coranderrk themselves.

      • annexedAn Act of Queensland · 1879
        “… the Islands described in the schedule hereto shall be annexed to and become part of the Colony of Queensland …”

        Source. Queensland Coast Islands Act 1879. Transcript: Documenting a Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy.

        What it meant

        Taken over and joined on to something bigger.

        What it did

        It made the Torres Strait Islands part of Queensland. The Mabo judgment of the High Court says it may be assumed the Meriam people knew nothing of it at the time.
